    A working guide for VYRO · 01

    From brief
    to final.

    How our team plans the work, produces it and gives useful feedback.

    Every handoff answers four questions.

    What are we making? Who owns it? What are we checking? Who can approve the next step?

    17 short sections · About 15–20 minutes, plus discussion. Use the lesson list to return to a template during a project.

    This is a proposed VYRO workflow for a small CGI studio. It uses the three lead roles discussed so far. Assign names, dates, fees and review rounds at the start of each project.

    02 · The whole process

    Decide, test, make, check.

    02 / Idea

    Starts with
    Agreed creative brief and useful research.
    Makes
    A selected concept: the main thought, story beats and reason it fits.
    Lead
    CD, with AD and early CGI input.
    Ready when
    CD approves the direction; client agrees where required.
    Next
    Develop board and look. Run bounded tests on risky ideas.

    03 / Board & look

    Starts with
    Selected concept, verified sources and agreed primary format.
    Makes
    Storyboard, representative styleframes, still compositions and timed animatic.
    Lead
    AD leads visual development with named makers.
    Ready when
    CD approves story/look/timing after AD review and CGI feasibility input.
    Next
    Prepare detailed tasks. Resolve any decision that affects the build.

    04 / Test & plan

    Starts with
    Developed board, look and timing; known technical questions.
    Makes
    Simple camera/look tests, shot tasks, asset list, estimates and production brief.
    Lead
    CGI lead with AD; coordinator records scope.
    Ready when
    Technical approach, scope, names, dates, outputs and review rounds are agreed.
    Next
    Begin full production. Keep approved inputs linked to each task.

    05 / Build

    Starts with
    Approved direction and production plan.
    Makes
    Checked assets; camera and motion previews; lighting/material tests; shot renders.
    Lead
    Named artists; CGI lead coordinates; AD reviews the look.
    Ready when
    Approve the relevant camera, action and look versions before long renders.
    Next
    Render the agreed shots and finish the sequence.

    06 / Finish

    Starts with
    Approved shot versions, copy and audio plan.
    Makes
    Composite, edit, sound, text and exported masters.
    Lead
    Named editor/compositor/sound makers; AD + CGI lead check; CD signs off creative work.
    Ready when
    Review the entire exported film and stills. Obtain required client approval.
    Next
    Package approved masters and agreed source files.

    07 / Deliver

    Starts with
    Approved masters, asset list, usage information and approval record.
    Makes
    A clear handoff; archived project; release checks when publishing is in scope.
    Lead
    Coordinator delivers; named publishing owner releases.
    Ready when
    Confirm the correct files arrived. Publishing owner checks assets in context.
    Next
    Review results against the goal and record lessons for the next project.
    The CGI lead joins early.

    They check difficult ideas while the art director develops the board and look. Small tests can happen before approval. Full production starts once the direction and production plan are agreed.

    Board and styleframe work can overlap. One approved shot can move forward while another is revised, if the change cannot affect it. Record that decision.

    03 · Who owns what

    Three leads. Different decisions.

    RoleOwnsHands overChecks
    Creative director
    CD    		Purpose, audience, central idea and the story the work tells.Creative brief; selected concept; decisions on the overall work.Does it answer the brief? Approves creative stages internally.
    Art director
    AD    		Visual direction: composition, world, material treatment, colour and lighting intention.Storyboard, styleframes and clear shot notes, made with artists as needed.Does each image follow the direction? Reviews the look through production.
    CGI leadTechnical approach, task assignment, scene consistency and reliable output.Feasibility tests, production plan, working scenes and checked renders.Can we make it within the agreed scope? Is the product and output technically correct?
    AD
    Defines what the light should reveal.    →Named lighting artist
    Builds and refines the light.    →CGI lead + AD
    Check technical quality and visual intent.

    The CGI lead may also execute lighting if assigned. The title alone does not give them every lighting task. If the AD builds a lighting test, record that assignment too.

    Who handles the schedule, client and extra specialists?

    Name a production coordinator. Until a producer is assigned, the proposed default is the CD coordinating reviews and client decisions; the CGI lead estimates technical work. Assign editing, sound, modelling, animation, lighting and compositing by name. A lead can wear more than one hat.

    The client supplies or confirms product facts and required claims, and gives client approval where agreed. A studio portfolio project uses a named internal approver. Publishing also needs a named owner.

    Role background: ScreenSkills on art direction, CG supervision and lighting. The split above is our proposed small-team arrangement; titles vary between studios.

    04 · Keep the decisions connected

    One project. Three useful briefs.

    CD → team

    Creative brief

    Why are we making this, who is it for and what must it communicate?

    Start with the problem. Develop and approve the idea through the concept stage.

    CD + AD → visual team

    Art & storyboard brief

    What should we see, in what order, and why?

    Turn the selected idea into beats, images and a direction for the look.

    AD + CGI lead → makers

    Production brief

    How will the agreed direction be built, checked and delivered?

    Include shot tasks, source files, tests, specifications and dependencies.

    Use the same project ID, shot IDs and version links.

    These can be three sections in one shared document. Update the relevant section when a decision changes. Keep earlier approved versions.

    DocumentIts job
    Reference boardShows examples and explains the principle to study.
    BriefStates the work, its reason, constraints and decisions.
    Scope agreementRecords outputs, fees, dates, included revisions and change handling.

    05 · What a brief can look like

    Start with a page people can use.

    Fictional teaching example: LUMA, a desk lamp. The facts, timing and outputs below are invented for this lesson.

    LUMA / A place to focusCreative brief v01 · Draft example
    Purpose & audience
    Help people choosing a lamp for a small home workspace picture it on their own desk.
    Takeaway & evidence
    A defined pool of light makes a small work area feel ready to use. Confirm the real lamp’s size, finish and light behaviour before making product claims.
    Selected idea
    Follow a pool of light from the task on the desk to the lamp that creates it. This field is added after concept approval.
    Outputs & use
    One 12-second, 16:9 film for a website; one complete-product still; two detail stills. Final hold says “Explore LUMA” and names the product.
    Must keep
    Correct proportions and finish; credible light; readable name. Any unresolved product fact stays marked as a question.
    Limits & decisions
    One desk setting. No character animation. Budget, dates, formats beyond 16:9 and included review rounds must be agreed before full production.
    Owners & success
    CD owns direction; AD owns look; CGI lead owns the build. Test whether viewers can recognise the lamp and explain the scene’s main idea; the publishing owner reviews product-page visits after release.

    The brief defines the job. It leaves room for the visual team to find a good way to do it. A new product claim needs evidence and approval.

    06 · Give the visual team a clear job

    Describe the discovery in each beat.

    LUMA / Art & storyboard briefBased on creative brief v01
    Story
    Notice the lit task → connect the beam to its source → recognise the complete lamp on the desk.
    Visual direction
    A small, lived-in desk with space around the lamp. Warm task light, a quieter background and a legible product outline.
    Reference notes
    For each link: name the useful feature, explain its job in this story and say what we will adapt. For example, study how a side light reveals an edge; choose a composition suited to this lamp.
    What the AD develops
    Rough thumbnails; a numbered storyboard for the whole film; styleframes for the key looks; a timed animatic. For this example, try three styleframes—one per beat.
    Every shot explains
    Framing, action, camera movement or hold, lighting intention, transition, sound/text and audience takeaway.
    Early CGI question
    Can the camera move from the desk detail to the source without losing the beam, product shape or final framing? Test a simple scene.

    The AD can work with a storyboard artist, designer or editor. The CGI lead checks feasibility alongside development. The CD reviews the complete story and look.

    07 · Images + instructions

    The board should explain the shot.

    Use simple drawings in the intended frame shape. Number the shots and add enough notes for another person to understand them.

    1

    ID & timing
    S02 · 03–08s · 5 seconds.

    2

    Frame & action
    Medium view. The beam connects the desk to the lamp head.

    3

    Camera
    Slow pull-back. Label the arrow CAMERA; the lamp stays still.

    4

    Lighting intention
    Keep the pool of light readable; separate the shade from the background.

    5

    Transition & sound
    Cut from S01; cut to S03 on the same lamp position. Quiet room sound, no voiceover.

    6

    Takeaway
    The viewer understands where the task light comes from.

    One shot may need several panels to show an important change. A still image needs its own composition board, crop, text space and use; it does not need a film timeline.

    Practical references: StudioBinder’s storyboard guide and Boords’ four-panel template (PDF).

    08 · Three different checks

    A good image still needs a good sequence.

    Storyboard

    What happens?

    Shot order, framing, actions and transitions. Rough drawings are enough if they are clear.

    Styleframes

    What does it look like?

    Representative images close to the intended finish: light, materials, colour, composition and text.

    Animatic / previs

    How does it play?

    An animatic times the board with temporary sound. Rough 3D previs can test camera movement, space and continuity.

    Try the 12-second board timing.
    Select Play or move the slider. This highlights timed sketches; it is a timing demonstration.

    S01 · 0–3sS02 · 3–8sS03 · 8–12s

    0.0s / 12s · S01 · Notice the task.

    Review the whole film at its intended length.

    Does each discovery read? Is the ending long enough to understand? Check sound and text as well as images. Temporary music is labelled temporary; final music needs the agreed usage rights.

    References: Boords on boards and animatics; Simon Rankin’s motion design development process.

    09 · From direction to a build plan

    Hand over decisions and source files.

    AD sends

    Approved brief, board and animatic versions; styleframes; shot IDs; reference links with reasons; text layouts; product source material; open questions.

    CGI lead returns

    A shot breakdown, named makers, task order, estimates, required tests, delivery settings and any risks or missing information.

    LUMA / S02 production noteExample · Draft
    Creative target
    Use board v02, animatic v02 and styleframe S02 v03. Keep the warm pool and readable shade outline.
    Sources & build
    Confirm product dimensions, finish references and light behaviour with the product owner. Model to those sources; make desk and lamp assets reusable across shots.
    Test first
    Block camera and lamp with simple geometry. Check that the pull-back connects beam and source. Then test the shade edge, beam and shadow in a short rendered sample.
    Output & checks
    Example settings: 1920 × 1080, 24fps, 5s. S02 has 120 output frames; the CGI lead records the scene frame range, including any extra frames needed for editing. Check flicker, outline, product accuracy and continuity with S01/S03.
    Owners & questions
    Assign camera, lighting, render and composite artists by name. AD reviews the look; CGI lead checks the scene and output. Missing facts get an owner and a due date.

    Record renderer, colour settings, pass requirements, source-file delivery and frame numbering in the real production brief. Do not leave those choices to the last export.

    10 · Make the hard decisions while changes are cheap

    Build in passes.

    1. 1

      Check the product and rough scene.

      Verify shape, scale and source material. Block the camera and setting. Review framing before adding detail.

    2. 2

      Test movement and the look.

      Use previews for action, camera and timing. Develop materials and lighting against the styleframes. Review a representative shot before extending it across the film.

    3. 3

      Make the full sequence.

      Assign shot work. Review cuts and stills together for consistent scale, light and finish. Test selected rendered frames and short ranges before long renders.

    4. 4

      Render and finish.

      Render the approved shots. Composite the image layers, edit, add sound and text, and check the complete film. Export the agreed versions.

    Review at the quality needed for that decision.

    A grey preview can prove camera movement. A finished-looking frame can prove the look. A still cannot prove motion stability; check a moving render sample.

    This pass order is our proposed CGI workflow. The CGI lead adjusts the task plan for the job. Lightweight boards and tests still matter when the project is small.

    Write the decision down.

    “Storyboard v03 approved for shot order and framing by [name] on [date]. Timing approval remains pending on animatic v02.”

    Suggested statuses: Draft → In review → Changes needed → Approved. Use “Approved with conditions” only when the conditions, owner and affected work are explicit. Silence or a completed comment is not final approval.

    Internal checks happen before client review. Agree the client’s review stages at kickoff. Product facts, claims and publication permissions have their own required owners.

    12 · One round, one clear task list

    The loop ends with a checked decision.

    01

    Share

    The maker sends the version, purpose of review, known limits and deadline.

    02

    Review

    Reviewers point to a shot, frame or time and explain the problem.

    03

    Agree

    The review owner merges notes, resolves conflicts and assigns actions.

    04

    Revise

    The maker changes the work and sends a new version with a change summary.

    05

    Verify

    The reviewer checks the new version, closes fixed notes and records the stage decision.

    ↶ If the issue remains, return to an agreed action. If the direction changes, revisit the affected approval.
    Review ownerConsolidates
    ADImage, composition and lighting notes for artists.
    CGI leadTechnical tasks and estimates; flags dependencies.
    CD / coordinatorClient notes and conflicts about the idea or scope. Sends one agreed response.

    Keep comments in one shared review place. WhatsApp can carry the review link and reminder; copy decisions into the project record. Agree review deadlines and included rounds before production.

    Tool reference: Frame.io’s comments documentation. Consolidation and decision ownership above are proposed VYRO practice.

    13 · Point to the problem

    Write notes a maker can act on.

    “S02 v04, 00:06–00:07: the shade blends into the wall. We need to recognise the lamp here. Please test more separation while keeping the warm desk light.”

    A useful note includes the location, the observed problem, why it matters and the desired result. The maker can propose the technical fix.

    “Make it more premium.”
    Problem
    The word gives us no clear image to change.
    Decision
    CD/AD asks which quality should improve: material readability, composition, restraint or another specific issue.
    Agreed action
    After clarification, an agreed note might be: for S02 v04 at 00:06, test separation between shade and wall. Keep the warm desk pool.
    Follow-through
    AD owns the visual decision; the assigned artist proposes a test. Review that test against the stated result.

    “Darken the room.” / “Make the lamp easier to see.”
    Problem
    Two notes could lead the maker in different directions.
    Decision
    AD compares both with the approved look. A darker background may still allow a clearer lamp outline. If the notes change the idea, CD resolves it.
    Agreed action
    Keep the quiet room, but test local contrast around the shade. Send one agreed note with the relevant reference.
    Follow-through
    The maker receives one task. Conflicting notes stay visible in the log with the resolution recorded.
    “Can we add a different room and two extra shots?”
    Problem
    The request changes the agreed work.
    Decision
    CD confirms the purpose. CGI lead estimates assets, shots, rendering and any repeated approvals. Coordinator obtains agreement on cost and dates.
    Agreed action
    Write the added outputs and revised schedule into the scope record before assigning the new production work.
    Follow-through
    Continue unaffected approved tasks. Reopen the board, look or plan only where the new work changes them.
    Keep fixes and preferences visible.

    Mark an issue as required, an improvement to consider, or a scope change. The review owner decides priority. Ask about unclear notes before spending time on them.

    14 · A late change has a return path

    Find what the change affects.

    Correction / agreed revision

    Match the approved direction.

    Example: a wrong product colour. Assign the fix and check the affected shots. Log any schedule impact.

    Changed direction / scope

    Agree the new work.

    Example: a new room, added shot or extra format. CD confirms the reason; CGI lead estimates dependencies; coordinator gets agreement on cost and dates.

    Change request→Trace affected files and approvals→Agree impact→Revise + reapprove

    A new room may affect the board, styleframes, assets, lighting, animation and render schedule. Reopen the affected stages and freeze only the work that depends on them.

    15 · Finish the handoff

    Delivered and published are two steps.

    Check

    Watch the final files.

    Play every export. Check cut, flicker, text, logo, colour, sound and product accuracy. Check dimensions, duration and formats against the delivery list.

    Package

    Make the files easy to use.

    Include an asset list showing filename, purpose, format and version. Add agreed source files, usage information and the approval record.

    Release

    Name the publishing owner.

    They place the assets, check playback or crops in context and confirm the live links. Later, compare results with the original goal.

    Record what is actually ready.

    Delivery can be complete while release is pending. Keep an archive of the brief, direction, scenes, masters and decisions so the next adaptation can use the same foundation.

    Useful kickoff questions that prevent trouble later
    • Who gives final approval, and who combines client feedback?
    • Which outputs, formats, stills, source files and review rounds are included?
    • Who supplies product facts, copy, logos and permissions?
    • Who owns editing, sound, lighting and publishing?
    • Which missing decisions stop which tasks?
    • Where are reviews, versions and approvals recorded?

    Start with these working documents.
